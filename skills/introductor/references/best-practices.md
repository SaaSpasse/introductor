# Best practices - warm email introductions

## Research sources

Compiled from Fred Wilson (AVC), Mark Suster (Both Sides of the Table), Alex Iskold (Startup Hacks), Claire Carroll, CB Insights, David Vandegrift (4Degrees), and networking experts.

## The double opt-in protocol

Coined by Fred Wilson (2009). The golden rule of warm intros:

1. **Never introduce two people without asking both first.** An unwanted intro puts people in an awkward position.
2. Ask each person separately: "Would you like me to introduce you to [person]? Here's why I think it'd be valuable..."
3. Only proceed when both say yes.
4. If either declines, respect it silently - don't tell the other person who said no.

**Exception:** Very casual intros between close friends where you know both would be happy. Even then, err on the side of asking.

## The forwardable email (Alex Iskold)

When someone asks YOU for an intro, ask them to write a "forwardable email" - a blurb you can forward to the target. This:
- Reduces work for the connector
- Lets the requester frame themselves
- Gives the target enough context to decide

**Structure of a forwardable email:**
1. Who you are (name, company, URL) - 1 sentence
2. What you do (business/product) - 2-3 sentences
3. Why it's relevant to the target - 1-2 sentences
4. Traction/credibility - 1-2 data points
5. The ask - what you want from the intro

**Keep it under 250 words.**

## Structure of a great intro email

### The two-paragraph format

```
Hey [Person A],

Meet [Person B] - [1 sentence context]. [1-2 sentences on what makes B credible/interesting]. [Link to learn more].

[Person A], meet [Person B] - [same format, 1-2 sentences on A for B's benefit]. [Link].

I'll let you two take it from here - feel free to move me to BCC.
```

### The group intro format

When introducing one person to a team or multiple people:

```
Hey [Person A],

Suite à [context], je te connecte avec [description of the group].

[Person B] - [role + credibility nugget + link]

[Person C] - [role + credibility nugget + link]

[Why they work together / what they can do for Person A]

[BCC invitation]
```

## The BCC protocol

After the intro email is sent:
1. One of the introduced parties replies-all
2. They thank the introducer
3. They move the introducer to BCC ("moving you to BCC, thanks for the intro!")
4. The introduced parties continue their conversation privately

**Why BCC matters:** It's the best way to thank someone for an intro - spare them from the email thread that follows.

## Subject line best practices

- Be specific: "Intro: Sarah ↔ Mike - AI hiring tools" not "Introduction"
- Include both names so it's scannable in the inbox
- Add 3-4 words of context after the names
- Keep under 60 characters
- Formats that work:
  - "Intro: [Name A] ↔ [Name B] - [context]"
  - "Intro: [Name A] x [Name B] - [reason]"
  - "[Name A], meet [Name B] ([company])"

## What makes people respond to intros

- **Personalization** - 50% higher open rate with personalized subject lines
- **Credibility signals** - concrete numbers, known brands, relevant achievements
- **Brevity** - average email response rate is 8-10%; personalized intros can hit 40%+
- **Relevance** - the "why" of the intro is clear and compelling
- **Warm path** - warm intros have 3% no-show rate vs 25% for cold (Commsor data)

## Common mistakes

1. **No double opt-in** - introducing people who didn't ask to be introduced
2. **Too long** - writing a biography instead of a nugget
3. **No "why"** - not explaining why these two people should connect
4. **Generic subject** - "Introduction" tells the recipient nothing
5. **Raw URLs** - pasting full URLs instead of hyperlinking
6. **Wrong tone** - too formal ("Dear Sir/Madam") or too casual for the context
7. **Including signature** - email clients add these automatically
8. **Not checking facts** - stating something wrong about a person's role or company
9. **One-sided intro** - only describing one person, not giving context on both
10. **Forgetting the BCC reminder** - the other parties don't know the etiquette
